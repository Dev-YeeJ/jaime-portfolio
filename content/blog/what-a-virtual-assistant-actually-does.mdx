"Virtual assistant" is one of those job titles that tells you the delivery method and nothing about the job. It's like hiring someone as a "person who uses a computer." So here's the actual work, in the four shapes I do it, and what I think separates doing it well from doing it at all.

## 1. Customer support

The visible part is answering questions. The valuable part is everything around that.

Good support means a customer gets a clear answer, in the voice of the business, without having to explain their problem twice. It means knowing which questions I can close myself and which ones need to go to someone else — and escalating those quickly instead of sitting on them to look competent.

The part most people skip: **keeping a record of what keeps coming up.** If the same question arrives eleven times in a month, that's not eleven support tickets, that's one unwritten FAQ or one confusing button. Support that only answers is a treadmill. Support that reports back is a feedback loop.

## 2. Social media management

Scheduling posts is the easy half. The hard half is that a feed is a sequence, not a pile.

This is where my film background actually earns its keep. I have a background in film — including a Best Director win at the Orata Film Festival — and the thing directing teaches you is pacing: what you show first, what you hold back, how long you sit on a beat before cutting. A month of posts has the same problem. Five announcements in a row reads as noise. An announcement, then something useful, then something human, then the announcement's follow-up reads as a voice.

Practically, the work is:

- Planning a calendar so the account has a shape, not just uploads
- Writing captions that sound like the business and not like a caption
- Watching comments and DMs, because an unanswered comment on a public post is worse than no post
- Reporting what actually landed, so next month is informed rather than repeated

## 3. Email and calendar management

The goal of inbox management is a clean, unglamorous one: **you open your inbox and only see what needs you.**

Getting there means triage (what's urgent, what's routine, what's noise), drafted replies for the routine things so they only need a glance and a send, and filters and labels that hold up after three months instead of collapsing into a second inbox. On the calendar side it's scheduling, reminders, and protecting the blocks that shouldn't get booked over.

Nobody notices this work when it's done well. That's the point.

## 4. Data entry

Data entry has a reputation as the mindless part. It isn't, and the difference shows up later.

Entering records accurately is the baseline. The actual skill is what you do with the rows that don't fit: a duplicate with a slightly different spelling, a date in the wrong format, a total that doesn't reconcile. The wrong instinct is to enter it as-is and move on, because that's faster and the mess becomes someone else's problem in three months. The right one is to normalise what's clearly normalisable, flag what isn't, and tell someone.

I care about this more than most because I've been on the other end of it. Building a barangay information system means designing around resident and household records, document requests, blotter reports and financial transactions — and every one of those tables is only as trustworthy as whoever typed into it.

## Why I'm good at it

Three reasons, and none of them are "I'm organised," because everyone says that.

**I've held roles where dropping something is public.** I'm the Public Relations Officer for the Junior Information Technology Society, and I've been an elected Sangguniang Kabataan councilor since 2023. Both are jobs where follow-through is visible and forgetting is a thing people notice. That's a different pressure from a to-do list only you can see.

**I understand the systems underneath.** I build web and mobile applications. When I'm doing data entry in an admin panel or triaging tickets in a helpdesk, I know what the software is doing with what I type, where it's likely to break, and what's worth reporting as a bug rather than working around forever.

**I write.** Most of this job is writing — replies, captions, summaries, notes. Doing it clearly and in someone else's voice is a skill, and it's the one I'd point at first.
